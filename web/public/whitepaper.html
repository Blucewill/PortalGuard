    PortalGuard

    Capped, expiring and visible token permissions for the Portaldot network.

    Published
    31 October 2026
    Author
    Blucewill
    Network
    Portaldot V3 testnet
    Status
    Deployed, unaudited

    Abstract

    Wallet drainers took about 494 million dollars from more than 332,000 wallets in 2024. Most of those losses did not come from broken cryptography. They came from permissions: a victim approved a contract to spend their tokens, and the attacker used that approval to empty the wallet.

    PortalGuard is a set of four tools that protect Portaldot users at each stage of that attack. PortalGuard Shield, a browser extension, warns users before they sign a risky permission. SafeApprove, a smart contract, replaces unlimited approvals with grants that have a spending cap and an expiry time. The approval scanner shows every permission a wallet has given and revokes any of them in one click. The DrainerRegistry is a shared, on-chain list of known drainer addresses that the other three tools consult.

    This paper describes the problem, the design of each component, the deployment on Portaldot V3 testnet, the results of testing, and the known limitations.

    1Introduction

    Token permissions are one of the basic building blocks of on-chain applications. A decentralised exchange, a marketplace or a lending app cannot move a user's tokens unless the user first gives it permission. That permission, called an approval or an allowance, is recorded on the token contract and stays in force until the user removes it.

    The model is powerful, but it was designed for developers, not for the people who sign the requests. Wallets display approvals as technical prompts. Applications routinely ask for unlimited amounts so that users never have to approve twice. Approvals have no expiry date. And once granted, they are invisible unless the user knows how to search the blockchain for them.

    Wallet drainers exploit every one of those gaps. PortalGuard closes them for Portaldot, a new network that can build safety in from the start instead of adding it after the first wave of losses.

    This paper is written for three audiences: users who want to understand how PortalGuard protects them, developers who may integrate SafeApprove into their applications, and reviewers assessing the design. Sections 2 and 3 explain the problem. Sections 4 to 9 describe the design. Sections 10 to 12 cover deployment, evaluation and limitations.

    2How token permissions work

    Three permission types account for almost all approval-based theft. Each one lets a third party move assets out of a wallet without asking the owner again.

    2.1 Token allowances

    Fungible tokens on EVM networks follow the ERC-20 standard. A holder calls approve(spender, amount) on the token contract to let a spender move up to that amount. The spender then calls transferFrom(owner, recipient, amount) whenever it wants, with no further signature from the owner.

    To save users from approving again later, many applications request the largest possible number, 2 to the power of 256 minus 1. In practice this is an unlimited allowance. It covers every token the user holds today and every token they receive in the future.

    2.2 NFT operator approvals

    NFT standards provide setApprovalForAll(operator, true). One call gives the operator control of every NFT the owner holds in that collection, including items acquired later.

    2.3 Permit signatures

    Permit messages, defined in EIP-2612 and extended by Permit2, grant an allowance through an off-chain signature instead of a transaction. The user pays no gas and sees no transaction, only a request to sign a message. Many users read this as a harmless login. Anyone holding the signature can submit it on-chain and receive the allowance.

    2.4 Why users cannot see the risk

    Wallet confirmation screens show contract addresses, hexadecimal data and a "spending cap" field. They do not explain that the user is handing over control of their balance, they do not show how long the permission lasts, and once the user confirms, the permission disappears from view. Users cannot protect themselves from something they cannot see or understand.

    3The problem: approval drainers

    3.1 Anatomy of an attack

    An approval drainer follows the same sequence in almost every case:

    1. The attacker builds a convincing website: a fake airdrop, a free mint, a reward claim or a copy of a real application.
    2. The victim clicks a button such as "Claim". Behind it, the site requests an unlimited approval, an operator approval or a Permit signature for a contract the attacker controls.
    3. The wallet shows a technical prompt. The victim confirms.
    4. The attacker calls transferFrom and moves the tokens out, either immediately or weeks later when the balance is larger.

    The victim never sends a transaction to the attacker. This is why the typical complaint is: "I only clicked claim. I never sent anything. How is my wallet empty?"

    3.2 Scale

    Industry reports and news coverage show that this is not a rare edge case.

    • In 2024, wallet drainers stole 494 million dollars from 332,000 wallets, an increase of 67 percent over 2023. More than half of the large losses, 56.7 percent, came from a single signed Permit approval. (Scam Sniffer)
    • In August 2024, one holder lost 55 million dollars in DAI after signing one phishing transaction. (The Block)
    • Between May 2021 and December 2023, approval phishing alone accounted for about 1 billion dollars in losses. (Chainalysis)
    • In 2025, a quieter year, phishing still cost 106,000 victims 83.85 million dollars. (Scam Sniffer)
    • In October 2026, US prosecutors charged six people over fake NFT websites that used wallet permissions to drain about 517,000 dollars from 74 wallets. In one case, a victim's 9,067 USDT was gone about 33 minutes after it was deposited. (Yahoo News, The Defiant)

    3.3 Why losses are permanent

    Blockchain transfers are final. There is no bank to call, no chargeback and no central party that can reverse a transaction. Revoking an approval after the theft stops further losses, but it does not return what was already taken. Prevention is the only effective defence.

    On average, each wallet in the 2024 figures lost about 1,490 dollars. For a first-time user, one loss is often enough to leave a network for good.

    6SafeApprove

    SafeApprove is the key component of PortalGuard. A warning can be ignored, and a revoke only helps if the user notices in time. A cap protects the user even when they have already been fooled.

    6.1 The grant model

    The user approves the SafeApprove contract once for a token. After that, instead of approving applications directly, the user gives each application a grant. A grant has three properties: the maximum amount the application may still collect, the time at which the grant expires, and the lockdown epoch in which it was created. Grants are stored per owner, per token and per spender.

    // SafeApprove.sol
struct Grant {
    uint256 amount;    // tokens the spender may still pull
    uint64  expiresAt; // unix timestamp after which the grant is dead
    uint64  epoch;     // owner's epoch when granted; lockdown() bumps it
}

// owner => token => spender => grant
mapping(address => mapping(address => mapping(address => Grant))) public grants;

    6.2 Collecting tokens

    An application collects tokens by calling spend(owner, token, to, amount). The contract runs four checks, in this order, and stops at the first failure:

    1. The grant exists and was created in the owner's current epoch. Otherwise the call fails with GrantRevoked.
    2. The grant has not expired. Otherwise it fails with GrantExpired.
    3. The caller is not flagged in the DrainerRegistry. Otherwise it fails with SpenderFlagged.
    4. The requested amount fits within what is left of the cap. Otherwise it fails with ExceedsAllowance.

    If all checks pass, the contract reduces the remaining amount first and only then transfers the tokens, following the checks-effects-interactions pattern. The tokens move directly from the owner to the recipient. SafeApprove never holds them.

    6.3 Revoking and lockdown

    A user can cancel one grant with revoke or several at once with revokeMany. For emergencies, lockdown increases the user's epoch number by one. Every grant created in an earlier epoch becomes invalid immediately. Because the contract compares epochs instead of deleting grants one by one, lockdown costs the same small amount of gas whether the user has two grants or two thousand.

    Table 3. SafeApprove interface
    FunctionCallerEffect
    grant(token, spender, amount, expiresAt)OwnerCreates or replaces a grant. Fails if the expiry is in the past or the spender is flagged.
    available(owner, token, spender)AnyoneReturns the amount a spender can collect now, or 0 if the grant is expired, revoked or flagged.
    spend(owner, token, to, amount)ApplicationCollects tokens within the grant.
    revoke(token, spender)OwnerCancels one grant.
    revokeMany(keys)OwnerCancels several grants in one transaction.
    lockdown()OwnerCancels every grant the owner has made.

    6.4 Security properties

    • Bounded loss. A malicious application can take at most the remaining amount of its grant, and only before the expiry time.
    • Flagged spenders are blocked twice. The registry is checked when the grant is created and again on every spend, so a spender flagged after receiving a grant is stopped too.
    • No custody. Funds stay in the owner's wallet until the moment of a valid transfer.
    • One trusted contract instead of many. The user gives a single unlimited approval, to SafeApprove, instead of one to every application. The trust is concentrated in a small contract whose code is public and can be audited.

    7DrainerRegistry

    The DrainerRegistry is a public list of addresses known to drain wallets. When one user is attacked, every other user should be protected from the same address. The registry makes that knowledge available on-chain, where any wallet or application can read it.

    Anyone can report a suspicious address with report(suspect, reason). Each reporter can report a given address only once, and the contract keeps a count of reports. A report on its own does not block anything. The registry owner reviews reports and confirms an address with flag(suspect, reason). A flag added by mistake is removed with unflag(suspect). Every action emits an event, so the history of the list is public.

    SafeApprove reads the registry when grants are created and spent, the scanner reads it when rating approvals, and Shield reads it before the wallet opens. In the current version the registry has a single owner. Section 13 describes the move to several reviewers.

    8Approval scanner

    The scanner answers one question: who can spend my tokens right now? It works for any address, with or without a connected wallet, and finds every approval, including ones made long before PortalGuard existed.

    8.1 Finding approvals

    The scanner requests every Approval and ApprovalForAll event in which the address is the owner, using the standard eth_getLogs call. Some RPC providers limit how many blocks one query may cover. When a query is refused, the scanner splits the block range in half and retries each half, down to ranges of about 1,000 blocks.

    8.2 Checking what is still active

    An event shows that an approval was given, not that it still exists. It may already have been used up or revoked. For each spender found, the scanner reads the current state from the token contract with allowance, getApproved or isApprovedForAll, and keeps only the approvals that are still active.

    8.3 Rating risk

    Each active approval receives one of four ratings. The list is sorted by risk, then by how recently the approval was given.

    Table 4. Risk rules
    RatingRuleMessage shown
    DangerSpender is flagged in the DrainerRegistryReported drainer. Revoke now.
    HighOperator approval for a whole NFT collectionCan move every NFT in this collection.
    HighAllowance of at least half the maximum valueUnlimited amount, never expires.
    MediumSpender is a personal wallet, not a contractSpender is a personal wallet, not an app.
    LowLimited amount to a contractLimited amount to a contract.

    8.4 Revoking

    Each approval has a Revoke button. It asks the user's wallet to sign approve(spender, 0) for tokens or setApprovalForAll(operator, false) for NFT collections. A scan can be shared as a link by adding the address to the page URL, so a user can ask a friend to check their wallet.

    9PortalGuard Shield

    Most victims cannot read a wallet confirmation screen, so the warning must arrive before that screen appears and must be written for a non-technical reader. Shield is a browser extension that does exactly that.

    9.1 Architecture

    Websites talk to a wallet through the wallet's request function, defined in EIP-1193. Shield is a Manifest V3 extension with three parts:

    • A content script runs inside each page, next to the website's own code, as soon as the page starts loading. It wraps the request function of every wallet it finds, both the classic window.ethereum object and wallets that announce themselves through EIP-6963.
    • A bridge script relays messages between the page and the extension, because the page cannot talk to the extension directly.
    • A background service worker looks up spenders in the DrainerRegistry and keeps count of warnings shown and requests cancelled.

    9.2 What Shield detects

    Table 5. Detection rules
    RequestConditionShield response
    approve or increaseAllowanceAmount of at least 2 to the power of 128High-risk warning: unlimited access
    approve or increaseAllowanceAny smaller, non-zero amountCaution: check that you trust this site
    setApprovalForAllTurning approval onHigh-risk warning: access to all NFTs
    eth_signTypedData_v3 or v4Message type contains "Permit"High-risk warning: this is not a login
    Any of the aboveSpender flagged in the DrainerRegistryRed warning: known wallet drainer

    Two kinds of request pass straight through, because they make the user safer: revokes, which set an amount to zero or turn an approval off, and grants to SafeApprove on Portaldot.

    9.3 The user's choice

    When Shield pauses a request, it explains in plain words what the site is asking for, who would receive the permission and what could happen. The user then chooses. Cancel, the recommended option, stops the request and returns the standard "user rejected" error, code 4001, so the website behaves as if the user had declined in the wallet. The wallet never opens. "I trust this site, continue to my wallet" passes the request to the wallet unchanged.

    11Evaluation

    11.1 Automated tests

    The contracts have 14 automated tests, run with Hardhat on a local chain. They cover grants, caps, expiry, revoking, lockdown, flagged spenders and the demo contracts. Shield's detection logic has 8 unit tests covering each request type, revokes and trusted spenders. Shield was also tested in a real Chrome browser with a simulated wallet, confirming that warnings appear, that Cancel returns code 4001 and that Continue forwards the request unchanged.

    11.2 Live demonstration

    The live demo runs a real approval attack on Portaldot testnet against the user's own wallet with 1,000 worthless test tokens. Table 8 shows the outcome in each scenario.

    12Security considerations and limitations

    PortalGuard reduces risk. It does not remove it. These are its known limits.

    • Shield is a safety net, not a guarantee. It runs in the same page as the website, so a determined malicious site could try to get around it. Building the same checks into wallets would close this gap.
    • SafeApprove requires adoption. Its protection applies only when an application collects tokens through it. Until applications adopt it, users rely on Shield and the scanner for ordinary approvals.
    • SafeApprove is a concentration of trust. Users give it an unlimited approval. A flaw in SafeApprove would affect every user, which is why it is deliberately small and why an audit is required before mainnet.
    • The registry has one owner. A single reviewer can make mistakes or become unavailable. Flags can be removed, and every change is public, but governance must move to several reviewers.
    • Revoking does not recover funds. It stops future theft only.
    • Testnet only. The contracts have not been audited and must not be used with real funds.

    13Future work

    • Wallet integration. Work with Portaldot wallet teams to show Shield's warnings inside the wallet itself, where a malicious page cannot interfere.
    • Application adoption. Provide a small developer kit so Portaldot applications can request SafeApprove grants instead of unlimited approvals.
    • Community review. Replace the single registry owner with several independent reviewers or a voting process.
    • Distribution. Publish Shield in the Chrome Web Store.
    • Audit and mainnet. Complete an independent security audit, then deploy to Portaldot mainnet.

    14Conclusion

    Approval drainers succeed because permissions are unlimited, permanent, invisible and explained badly. PortalGuard answers each of those weaknesses: Shield explains a request before it is signed, SafeApprove gives every permission a cap and an expiry, the scanner makes permissions visible and reversible, and the DrainerRegistry shares what one victim learns with everyone else. All four components are deployed and working on Portaldot V3 testnet today.

    Approve less. Lose nothing.

    15Glossary

    Allowance
    The amount of a token a spender is permitted to move from an owner's wallet.
    Approval
    The act of granting an allowance or operator permission, usually through a wallet confirmation.
    Drainer
    A malicious contract or website that tricks users into granting permissions, then uses them to take their assets.
    Epoch
    A counter kept by SafeApprove for each owner. Grants from an older epoch are invalid. Lockdown increases it by one.
    Grant
    A SafeApprove permission with a spending cap and an expiry time.
    Permit
    An off-chain signed message that creates an allowance without the user sending a transaction.
    Revoke
    To cancel a permission by setting its allowance to zero or turning operator approval off.
    Spender
    The address that receives permission to move tokens.
